HSBC Next-Generation Private Bank Core System: A Demand-Driven Functional Architecture

Deriving 18 modules, willingness to pay, and build sequence from 17 client pain points
Version v1.1 Date 2026-10-22 Demand basis nextgen-pb-real-people-cases v3.2 (17 personas) Capability benchmark nextgen-pb-unique-capabilities (Avaloq / Aladdin) External cross-check 16 institutional reports (see §0.5) Chinese 中文版 Nature Internal architecture proposal, not marketing material
This document answers three questions, not one.
First, why do clients feel pain? Starting from the words and failure scenarios of 17 personas, we distil eight Jobs.
Second, which pain will clients pay for? We distinguish “paid by the client”, “paid through credit or trading”, “paid by the bank”, and “pure infrastructure”. Need intensity is not the same as willingness to pay.
Third, what should the system look like, and what do we build first? 18 modules, 7 layers, 4 payment tiers, and 3 build waves.
What we do not do: repeat the vendor feature comparison of the earlier “Unique Capabilities” paper; restate the 17 cases; or produce market sizing without evidence. Scores are ordinal (1–5), for sequencing, not for pricing.

§0 How to read this: why start from pain, not from competitors

0.1 The earlier version was competitor-first; this one is demand-first

The earlier Unique Capabilities paper asked first: what does Avaloq have, what does Aladdin have, what can HSBC build? It answered “can we win?” This version asks first: at which moments will the 17 clients pay, why do they switch banks, why do they stay? It answers “is it worth building, what do we build first, and who pays?”

Both questions matter, but the order cannot be reversed. Start with competitors and you get a feature comparison table. Start with pain and you get a value chain. A feature table tells you where you can differentiate; a value chain tells you whether that differentiation can be charged for.

0.2 Three judgments

JudgmentMeaningDirect consequence for the architecture
Clients do not buy modules; they buy outcomesNo client pays for “client master data”, “IBOR”, or “workflow”. They pay for liquidity, certainty, continuity, and being understood.Modules must hang off Jobs, not off a feature list.
Need intensity ≠ willingness to payThe sharpest pain is usually compliance, evidence, and data consistency — the COGS the bank must bear. The willingness to pay clusters around moments with a transaction, a counterparty, or a deadline.The architecture must separate revenue modules from cost modules, and fund the latter with the former.
The value of a core system is deliverabilityNot the number of features, but whether high-WTP services can be delivered, proven, and scaled.“Evidence and as-of” is not a peripheral feature; it is the foundation of every high-value module.

Evidence: the “scenario · inference” and six-question sections of the 17 personas; the earlier capability assessment; public capabilities of Avaloq / Aladdin / Addepar. Scores are ordinal judgments, not market research; before formal approval they must be calibrated through 10–15 client interviews and 5 RM workshops.

0.4 One thread

17 personas → 8 Jobs → 18 modules → 4 payment tiers → 3 build waves.
The point of this thread is not “doing more”, but turning the four moments clients will pay most for — identity change, authority change, liquidity change, structure change — into deliverable services; every other module exists to make those four moments deliverable.

§2 Need intensity: which pain is deepest, most frequent, least substitutable

2.1 Pain is not evenly distributed

Across the 17 cases, what really keeps clients awake is not day-to-day allocation but four kinds of moment of change: identity change (migration, IPO, delisting, nationality), authority change (incapacity, death, dispute, succession), liquidity change (monetisation, pledge, credit, tax due), and structure change (trust, entity, separation, foundation). Day-to-day allocation is low-frequency pain; moments of change are high-frequency fear. A next-generation core system that can only operate in the day-to-day will be absent on the day the client needs the bank most.

2.2 What the five deepest pains require the system to do

Pain 1 | Authority change. The system must answer: who is the client, who is the account holder, who is the beneficiary, who is the decision-maker, who is the guardian; when incapacity, death or dispute occurs, how the subject switches; which instructions are valid and which require dual signatures. This is not a “relationship field”; it is an authority state machine.

Pain 2 | Cross-jurisdiction compliance. The system must answer: in which jurisdictions is this person tax-resident, from which date, under which rulebook, and who files; before and after an identity change, how the same asset is apportioned and replayed. This is not a “tax table”; it is a state with a timeline.

Pain 3 | Multiple registries of one interest. The system must answer: are these two positions the same economic interest; what is the combined position after aggregating acting-in-concert parties; what windows, lock-ups and disclosure obligations apply on each side. This is not “multi-currency holdings”; it is an economic-interest object above the ISIN.

Pain 4 | Evidence and versions. The system must answer: which version of the trust deed, court order or trustee resolution was in force; what were the beneficiary's rights on that date; under which clause was this distribution made. This is not “document management”; it is the chain of legal effect.

Pain 5 | The family graph. The system must answer: which branches exist, who is key, who benefits, who is a minor, which are charitable entities; who has authority, guardianship or a conflict of interest with whom. This is not “household relationships”; it is a family graph plus three sets of boundaries.

2.3 The counter-intuitive conclusion about need intensity

The deepest need is often not the need clients will pay most for. Authority, compliance, evidence and data consistency are “something goes wrong without it” needs that clients assume the bank should handle; pre-IPO liquidity, family governance and tax coordination are “better with it” needs for which clients will pay a premium or bear credit cost. The architecture must serve both; the business model must distinguish them.

Three common misjudgements about willingness to pay.
Misjudgement 1: treating “strong need” as “willing to pay”. Compliance, evidence and data consistency are intensely needed, but clients regard them as the bank's duty; trying to charge separately invites immediate price comparison.
Misjudgement 2: treating “the client will pay” as “the bank can charge”. The payer for family governance or tax coordination is often the lawyer or accountant; the bank can charge a coordination fee, not an opinion fee. The boundary must be set first.
Misjudgement 3: treating “high net worth” as “high willingness to pay”. A pre-IPO client may have very high paper wealth but low cash flow; price against spendable wealth, not against a rich-list ranking.

4.4 What each of the seven layers solves

L1 Client and family
Answers “who is the client, who is this family, who has authority”. Without this layer, every structure, asset and tax object downstream hangs off the wrong subject.
L2 Structures, ownership and assets
Answers “which entity holds the money, who owns which rights, what those rights are worth”. This is the dividing line between private banking and retail banking.
L3 Products and liquidity
Answers “how to invest, trade, borrow and pay”. Mostly table stakes: buy or partner, do not build.
L4 Tax, regulatory and obligations
Answers “which jurisdiction, which day, which rulebook, what falls due”. The only product-level differentiator in the earlier assessment, and the defensive core of this architecture. The industry phrasing is BCG's cross-border tax and legal navigation — clients want navigability across tax and legal regimes, not a tax opinion. 〔BCG 2026 p.19〕
L5 Operations and evidence
Answers “who does it, how far it has got, how it is proven”. Generates no revenue, but determines whether revenue modules are accepted by regulators and clients.
L6 Data, AI and integration
Answers “where facts come from, how they are reused, how the RM moves faster”. The foundation for scale and cost-to-serve.

What the six have in common: each occurs at a moment when the client must decide and the cost of getting it wrong is high. Clients are not paying for a feature; they are paying for someone to get this right on their behalf and be able to prove it. This is also why T1 modules must ship alongside T3 evidence/data modules: without evidence, a paid service becomes indefensible advice.

§6 Comparison with the earlier Unique Capabilities paper: one correction, two reinforcements, three concessions

6.1 The earlier conclusion

The earlier conclusion was: the only product-level capability that “Avaloq does not have, Aladdin does not have, and HSBC can build” is one item — a cross-jurisdiction client engine (identity/tax-residency/CRS-FATCA state machine + multi-jurisdiction tax computation and filing). The other three are non-product capabilities: falsifiable correctness infrastructure, advisor-oversight AI, and proprietary interfaces. On feature count, there is no win.

6.2 What the demand evidence corrects: not one item, but three differentiation clusters

The earlier paper judged at the product feature layer: does Avaloq have this field, does Aladdin have this module. The demand evidence judges at the client Job layer: at which moment will the client pay, and at which moment is there no substitute. The two conclusions are not identical.

§7 Build sequence: three waves and a Minimum Lovable Core

7.1 Principles for sequencing

  • Build the foundation for “moments of change” first: authority, structure, ownership, private equity, identity, tax and evidence — these seven decide whether the client stays at the critical moment.
  • Integrate commodity modules; do not build them: IBOR, trading, payments and risk analytics should reuse what exists or a partner; do not rewrite.
  • Every wave must independently deliver one client outcome: not “finish a layer”, but “let one type of client complete one thing”.
  • Evidence and the data platform come first: without M16/M17, the T1 modules cannot prove anything to a regulator or a client.

7.2 Minimum Lovable Core

If we could do only one thing, do this: give a cross-border family an executable, provable answer at the two moments of “authority change + liquidity change”.

  • Input: six family members, two jurisdictions, three entities, one private-equity position, one tax liability falling due.
  • Output: who has authority to instruct; which assets are pledgeable/spendable; what obligations fall due in the next 90 days; the basis and owner for each answer.
  • Minimum module set: M01 + M03 + M04 + M05 + M06 + M10 + M12 + M13 (CN/HK) + M14 + M16 + M17.
  • Not doing: full risk analytics, full held-away aggregation, digital assets, a generic AI assistant, an omnichannel portal.

Once this core exists, M02 (family governance), M07 (assets), M15 (cases) and M18 (AI) become meaningful; otherwise they are just more expensive reports.

8.2 How revenue modules fund cost modules

A simplified value flow: credit spread from M06/M10 + advisory/coordination fees from M02/M03/M05/M13 → cover the cost of M01/M07/M08/M09/M11/M14/M15/M16/M17 → what remains is the return on the system. Build only the T3 cost modules and the system becomes a more expensive back office; build only the T1 revenue modules and delivery stays manual and cannot scale.

Therefore budget should not be cut by “module” but by “client outcome”: “let a pre-IPO founder complete a liquidity arrangement without selling shares” requires seven modules — M05+M06+M10+M12+M13+M16+M17. Budgeting M06 alone cannot deliver it.

9.2 Validation required before launch

  1. 10–15 client interviews: at least 3 clients per T1 module; ask “what did you last pay for this, and to whom”, not “do you think it matters”.
  2. 5 RM/PA workshops: walk real cases through authority change/liquidity change/structure change; record what the system lacks and what people compensate for.
  3. Clause-by-clause tax/legal acceptance in 3 jurisdictions: CN/HK/SG first; UK/CH/AE in phase two.
  4. 3–5 pilot families: at least one pre-IPO, one family business and one cross-border family; deliver one provable outcome within 90 days.
  5. Cost-to-serve baseline: measure current RM/PA hours, reconciliation breaks and error rates before discussing ROI.

9.3 When this architecture should be overturned

  • If client interviews show that willingness to pay for T1 modules is lower than for T3 (clients will not pay a premium for governance/liquidity), the revenue model must be rewritten.
  • If Avaloq Mosaic delivers usable CN/HK tax coverage within 12 months, M13's uniqueness is downgraded and the architecture should shift to M02/M03/M06.
  • If HSBC internal data show that 80% of clients are below US$5m — the segment BCG calls structurally underserved — the module priorities in §4 must shift down and T1 “family governance” must give way to “low-cost service”.
  • If regulators determine that private-share pledge is impermissible or too capital-intensive, the M06/M10 revenue assumption fails.

§10 Conclusion: the architecture in one sentence

The HSBC next-generation Private Bank Core System is not a “better account system” but a “family operating system”: it knows who you are, what you own, who has authority to decide, what must happen next, and how to prove it.
Three engines: liquidity (M06/M10), continuity (M02/M03/M05), compliance (M12/M13/M14/M16).
Three ways to be paid: clients pay for outcomes (advisory/credit/tax coordination), clients pay for retention (family view/authority/evidence), the bank pays for cost and risk (data/workflow/AI).

The final answer to “how much is each module needed, and how much will clients pay”: the most needed are authority, compliance, evidence and data consistency; the most paid-for are liquidity, family governance, tax coordination and private equity. They are not the same list. The value of the architecture is that it builds the six modules clients will pay most for on top of the twelve modules they need most but cannot be charged for.

If the question is “what do we build first”, the answer is: build the chain of authority + structure + ownership + private equity + identity + tax + evidence, so that a cross-border family gets an executable, provable answer at the three moments of incapacity, IPO and migration. Everything else: buy where possible, partner where possible, do not build.
